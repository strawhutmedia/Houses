Before you buy a cheap auction house

A $1–$25K house is real — but "cheap" almost always means you're buying a project with legal homework. The price reflects title risk, condition, and location, not free money. Here's exactly what to check before you bid, in plain English. (This is general education, not legal advice — verify with the county and a local attorney.)

The one-line truth: the people who win at this treat it like a business — they verify the title, call the city, and budget the true all-in cost before bidding. Do that and it can be a genuine steal. Skip it and you can inherit a money pit with a demolition lien.

✅ The 30-minute checklist (do this before every bid)

Tip: on any listing in EquityScout, tap it and use "Before you bid" — it opens these steps with lookup links for that property's city and state.

⚠️ The real risks, ranked

1. Title — the #1 danger

A cheap auction house often has a clouded title: old mortgages, unknown heirs, gaps in the ownership chain. If title isn't clean you may not be able to insure, finance, or resell it until you clear it with a quiet-title lawsuit — typically $1,500–$4,000 and a few months with a local attorney. Assume you'll need this unless a title company will insure it today.

2. Condition & the city — will you be forced to fix it?

You're usually not required to renovate. But if the city flagged it as vacant/blighted, you inherit code fines, weed/lawn citations, board-up orders, vacant-property registration, and possibly a condemnation or demolition order you'd pay for. Always call code enforcement first.

3. "As-is, sight unseen, cash, no take-backs"

No inspection contingency, no financing contingency, money usually due within days, plus a buyer's premium (often ~10%). A $401 bid can be ~$441+ before closing and recording costs.

4. Property taxes & carrying costs

On a low-value distressed house the annual tax is often a few hundred to ~$1–2K (verify with the county) — the rate can be high but the assessed value is low. The costs people forget are insurance (expensive/hard on a vacant house), securing it (squatters, theft, winterizing), utilities, and lawn/weeds the city fines you for.

5. Possession

Is someone inside — a squatter or holdover occupant? If so you may have to evict (time + money) before you can touch it.

📄 Deed types, quickly

  • Warranty deed — seller guarantees clean title. Best for you.
  • Quitclaim deed — "I give you whatever I have" — no guarantee. Common from private flippers; verify title separately.
  • Tax deed / sheriff's deed — from a government sale; often still needs a quiet-title action before it's insurable.

💵 Budget the TRUE all-in cost

Before you decide a price is a "deal," add up:

  • Winning bid + buyer's premium + closing/recording fees
  • Quiet title (~$1.5–4K) if title isn't already insurable
  • Rehab to make it livable/sellable (get a rough contractor number)
  • ~1 year of carry: taxes + insurance + utilities + securing it
  • Any surviving liens you agree to take on
Red flags to walk away from: title company won't insure it and won't say why · an open demolition order · occupied with an uncooperative occupant · flood-zone or fire-damaged with no permits · seller won't tell you the deed type.
